Hume’s account in Treatise 1.4.6 most securely undermines the claim that experience supplies an idea of a simple, enduring self; its positive explanation of personal identity remains less secure. Since no impression persists unchanged throughout life, and introspection reveals only particular perceptions, Hume finds no experiential basis for the invariant subject those perceptions supposedly belong to (§6, paras. 2–3). His description of the mind as a “bundle” accordingly excludes an independently apprehended bearer of perceptions, while his warning about the theatre metaphor prevents us from quietly restoring such a bearer as the stage on which they appear (para. 4). Yet these arguments do not by themselves establish the unrestricted metaphysical conclusion that no subject could exist: their immediate force concerns what experience reveals and what idea it can support. Hume’s further task is to explain our attribution of identity to changing perceptions through relations that facilitate transitions in thought. Memory reveals causal relations and enables us to extend personal identity beyond remembered episodes; it therefore cannot simply constitute identity through recollection alone (para. 20). The gradual weakening of these relations also leaves no precise standard for settling borderline identity claims (para. 21). The Appendix, however, exposes a difficulty deeper than such verbal indeterminacy: Hume cannot satisfactorily explain how distinct perceptions are united in thought or consciousness while maintaining that the mind perceives no real connection among distinct existences. This admission does not expressly withdraw his critique of a simple self, but it prevents the associative account from standing as a completed explanation of mental unity. The resulting position is thus asymmetrical: the challenge to an experientially warranted substantial self retains its stated grounds, whereas the explanation of how perceptions compose a mind remains explicitly unresolved.
