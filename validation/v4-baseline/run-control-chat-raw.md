Hume’s account of personal identity in Treatise 1.4.6 establishes that the self cannot be understood as a simple, enduring substance underlying our experiences, but must instead be conceived as a succession or bundle of distinct perceptions. His introspective argument is that whenever he examines what he calls himself, he encounters only particular perceptions, such as sensations, emotions, and thoughts, and never an impression of a constant and invariable self. The apparent continuity of personal identity is therefore explained not by an independently existing subject but by relations among successive perceptions, particularly resemblance and causation, which facilitate the imagination’s transition from one perception to another. Memory plays a central role in this process, not by producing identity itself, but by revealing connections among past perceptions and enabling us to extend the attribution of identity beyond what we explicitly remember. Since these relations admit of gradual variation, Hume further suggests that disputes about the precise boundaries of personal identity lack any determinate philosophical standard and are largely verbal. Nevertheless, the Appendix substantially qualifies the security of this account. Hume acknowledges that although he can establish the distinctness of individual perceptions, he cannot satisfactorily explain the principle that connects them into the unity we attribute to a mind. His difficulty arises from the apparent incompatibility of two commitments: that perceptions are distinct existences and that the mind cannot perceive any real connection among distinct existences. The relations that supposedly account for personal identity consequently risk explaining only the mind’s tendency to attribute unity, rather than establishing how such unity is possible. Hume therefore does not simply abandon his account, but confesses his inability to reconcile its underlying principles. The resulting position is best understood as a powerful critique of the substantial self and a provisional account of how personal identity is attributed, rather than a fully secured explanation of the unity and continuity of the mind.
