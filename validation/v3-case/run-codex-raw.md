**Direct｜直接版**

In Treatise 1.4.6, Hume argues that no impression grounds the idea of a simple, invariant self, and that introspection encounters particular perceptions rather than such a self. He presents the mind as their changing collection, without a separately conceived bearer or stage. His account concerns identity in thought or imagination, rather than our passionate concern for ourselves; treating subtle identity disputes as verbal does not settle every question about personhood. In the Appendix, Hume retains both the distinct existence of perceptions and our inability to perceive real connections between them, yet finds his explanation of their unity defective and finds no theory that satisfies him. He nevertheless does not declare the difficulty absolutely insuperable.

**Balanced｜均衡版**

Hume's account in Treatise 1.4.6 denies experiential grounding to the idea of a simple, invariant self: impressions vary, and introspection reveals particular perceptions. The mind is presented as their changing collection, without a separately conceived bearer or stage. Within his stated concern with identity in thought or imagination, rather than passionate self-concern, **memory chiefly supplies access to the succession of perceptions and discovers causal relations; identity can consequently extend to forgotten periods. Remembering every episode is therefore not a requirement for attributing identity across time. He also explains the indeterminacy of subtle identity disputes through the gradual weakening of relations and of the transitions they facilitate: these supply no justified threshold for assigning the term identity.** His characterization of those disputes as verbal does not settle every question about personhood. In the Appendix, however, Hume retains the distinct existence of perceptions and the mind's inability to perceive real connections between them, while finding his account of their connection defective and no theory that satisfies him. His refusal to declare the difficulty absolutely insuperable leaves open the possibility of a satisfactory hypothesis.

**Careful｜审慎版**

Read alongside the Appendix, Treatise 1.4.6 offers both a critique of the simple self and an account of our attribution of identity. Hume finds neither a constant impression corresponding to a simple, invariant self nor anything in introspection beyond particular perceptions; he presents the mind as their changing collection, without a separately conceived bearer or stage. His inquiry concerns identity in thought or imagination rather than passionate self-concern. Memory supplies access to perceptual succession and discovers causal relations, allowing identity to extend beyond remembered episodes. The gradual weakening of relations and the transitions they facilitate also explains why subtle identity disputes lack a determinate threshold; calling these disputes verbal does not settle every question about personhood. **These proposals can be read as explaining how we trace and attribute identity, rather than demonstrating the existence of an invariant bearer of experience.** In the Appendix, Hume retains both the distinct existence of perceptions and the mind's inability to perceive real connections between them, yet calls his account of their connection defective and finds no theory that satisfies him. **This objection concerns how successive perceptions are united in thought or consciousness; tracing a causal history beyond recollection does not by itself answer that question. His continued commitment to both principles makes this an acknowledged tension within his position, rather than a straightforward return to a substantial self.** He does not, however, pronounce the difficulty absolutely insuperable. **His response includes diffidence about his own judgments, suggesting that his admission should temper confidence in the explanatory account** without being converted into a demonstration that no solution is possible.
