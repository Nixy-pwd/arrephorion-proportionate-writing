Hume’s account in Treatise 1.4.6 provides a stronger basis for rejecting an experientially warranted conception of a simple, enduring self than for accepting his explanation of the unity of perceptions. No invariant impression supplies the idea of such a self, and introspection discloses particular perceptions rather than a subject distinguishable from them (§6, paras. 2–3). Although Hume advances the stronger claim that the mind consists solely of successive perceptions, the failure to encounter a simple subject does not by itself establish that exhaustive account of its constitution (para. 4). His constructive explanation concerns identity as attributed in thought or imagination, explicitly leaving aside identity as it concerns the passions and self-concern (para. 5). Within this scope, memory reveals causal relations among perceptions and enables identity to be extended beyond remembered episodes; it therefore does not wholly constitute personal identity (para. 20). The gradual variation of relations and transitions also explains why precise boundaries of identity lack a determinate standard, making disputes over those boundaries grammatical rather than substantive (para. 21). The Appendix, however, exposes a difficulty deeper than uncertainty about where to draw such boundaries: Hume cannot satisfactorily explain how distinct perceptions are united in thought or consciousness when no real connection between distinct existences is perceived. This admission neither restores a substantial self nor simply retracts the preceding critique, whose apparent evidential force he continues to acknowledge. It nevertheless leaves the positive account insecure, since appealing to connected perceptions cannot complete the explanation while their connection in consciousness remains unresolved. The resulting position is thus an enduring challenge to the simple self accompanied by an acknowledged failure to explain fully the unity attributed to its perceptual succession.
