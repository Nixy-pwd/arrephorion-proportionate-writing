# Blind scoring — six paragraphs on Hume's personal identity

Score six paragraphs, P1–P6, against the source passages and the scoring reference below. All six answer the same task: "Write one English paragraph for the discussion section of a philosophy paper. The paragraph assesses what Hume's account of personal identity in Treatise 1.4.6 establishes about the self, and how secure that account is in light of his Appendix. Base it on the supplied passages only." Intended reader: a graduate-level philosophy reader who knows Hume's general empiricism but has not read these passages closely.

You are not told how the paragraphs were produced. Do not guess. Score each paragraph on its own, against the reference, not against the other paragraphs.

## What to score, per paragraph

1. **Floor coverage:** for each of F1–F4, present / absent / not applicable (F3 applies if the paragraph states what Hume denies; F4 applies if it mentions memory). Quote the phrase that carries each present core.
2. **Content errors:** each error, with the quoted phrase and which reference item or source passage it conflicts with. Use the error list and the "distortion not allowed" column. Judge in context. Write "none" if none.
3. **Repeated caveats:** any limitation stated more than once in the same paragraph in different words. Quote both statements. Write "none" if none.

Do not score style, length, or overall quality. Do not rewrite the paragraphs.

## Output

One table with a row per paragraph: P# | F1 | F2 | F3 | F4 | content errors (count) | repeated caveats (count). Then the quotations and reasons for every entry, paragraph by paragraph.

## Source passages

# Source: David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40), Book 1, Part 4, Section 6 "Of Personal Identity", and the Appendix (1740)

Excerpts, in order. Omitted paragraphs are not marked within the text.

**[Treatise 1.4.6, paragraph 2]**

Unluckily all these positive assertions are contrary to that very experience, which is pleaded for them, nor have we any idea of self, after the manner it is here explained. For from what impression could this idea be derived? This question it is impossible to answer without a manifest contradiction and absurdity; and yet it is a question, which must necessarily be answered, if we would have the idea of self pass for clear and intelligible. It must be some one impression, that gives rise to every real idea. But self or person is not any one impression, but that to which our several impressions and ideas are supposed to have a reference. If any impression gives rise to the idea of self, that impression must continue invariably the same, through the whole course of our lives; since self is supposed to exist after that manner. But there is no impression constant and invariable. Pain and pleasure, grief and joy, passions and sensations succeed each other, and never all exist at the same time. It cannot, therefore, be from any of these impressions, or from any other, that the idea of self is derived; and consequently there is no such idea.

**[Treatise 1.4.6, paragraph 3]**

But farther, what must become of all our particular perceptions upon this hypothesis? All these are different, and distinguishable, and separable from each other, and may be separately considered, and may exist separately, and have no need of any thing to support their existence. After what manner, therefore, do they belong to self; and how are they connected with it? For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe anything but the perception. When my perceptions are removed for any time, as by sound sleep; so long am I insensible of myself, and may truly be said not to exist. And were all my perceptions removed by death, and could I neither think, nor feel, nor see, nor love, nor hate after the dissolution of my body, I should be entirely annihilated, nor do I conceive what is farther requisite to make me a perfect nonentity. If anyone, upon serious and unprejudiced reflection thinks he has a different notion of himself, I must confess I can reason no longer with him. All I can allow him is, that he may be in the right as well as I, and that we are essentially different in this particular. He may, perhaps, perceive something simple and continued, which he calls himself; though I am certain there is no such principle in me.

**[Treatise 1.4.6, paragraph 4]**

But setting aside some metaphysicians of this kind, I may venture to affirm of the rest of mankind, that they are nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement. Our eyes cannot turn in their sockets without varying our perceptions. Our thought is still more variable than our sight; and all our other senses and faculties contribute to this change; nor is there any single power of the soul, which remains unalterably the same, perhaps for one moment. The mind is a kind of theatre, where several perceptions successively make their appearance; pass, re-pass, glide away, and mingle in an infinite variety of postures and situations. There is properly no simplicity in it at one time, nor identity in different; whatever natural propensity we may have to imagine that simplicity and identity. The comparison of the theatre must not mislead us. They are the successive perceptions only, that constitute the mind; nor have we the most distant notion of the place, where these scenes are represented, or of the materials, of which it is composed.

**[Treatise 1.4.6, paragraph 5]**

What then gives us so great a propensity to ascribe an identity to these successive perceptions, and to suppose ourselves possessed of an invariable and uninterrupted existence through the whole course of our lives? In order to answer this question, we must distinguish betwixt personal identity, as it regards our thought or imagination, and as it regards our passions or the concern we take in ourselves. The first is our present subject; and to explain it perfectly we must take the matter pretty deep, and account for that identity, which we attribute to plants and animals; there being a great analogy betwixt it, and the identity of a self or person.

**[Treatise 1.4.6, paragraph 20]**

As a memory alone acquaints us with the continuance and extent of this succession of perceptions, it is to be considered, upon that account chiefly, as the source of personal identity. Had we no memory, we never should have any notion of causation, nor consequently of that chain of causes and effects, which constitute our self or person. But having once acquired this notion of causation from the memory, we can extend the same chain of causes, and consequently the identity of our persons beyond our memory, and can comprehend times, and circumstances, and actions, which we have entirely forgot, but suppose in general to have existed. For how few of our past actions are there, of which we have any memory? Who can tell me, for instance, what were his thoughts and actions on the 1st of January 1715, the 11th of March 1719, and the 3rd of August 1733? Or will he affirm, because he has entirely forgot the incidents of these days, that the present self is not the same person with the self of that time; and by that means overturn all the most established notions of personal identity? In this view, therefore, memory does not so much produce as discover personal identity, by showing us the relation of cause and effect among our different perceptions. It will be incumbent on those, who affirm that memory produces entirely our personal identity, to give a reason why we can thus extend our identity beyond our memory.

**[Treatise 1.4.6, paragraph 21]**

The whole of this doctrine leads us to a conclusion, which is of great importance in the present affair, viz. that all the nice and subtle questions concerning personal identity can never possibly be decided, and are to be regarded rather as grammatical than as philosophical difficulties. Identity depends on the relations of ideas; and these relations produce identity, by means of that easy transition they occasion. But as the relations, and the easiness of the transition may diminish by insensible degrees, we have no just standard, by which we can decide any dispute concerning the time, when they acquire or lose a title to the name of identity. All the disputes concerning the identity of connected objects are merely verbal, except so far as the relation of parts gives rise to some fiction or imaginary principle of union, as we have already observed.

**[Appendix, paragraph that begins "I had entertained some hopes"]**

I had entertained some hopes, that however deficient our theory of the intellectual world might be, it would be free from those contradictions, and absurdities, which seem to attend every explication, that human reason can give of the material world. But upon a more strict review of the section concerning personal identity, I find myself involved in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent. If this be not a good general reason for scepticism, it is at least a sufficient one (if I were not already abundantly supplied) for me to entertain a diffidence and modesty in all my decisions. I shall propose the arguments on both sides, beginning with those that induced me to deny the strict and proper identity and simplicity of a self or thinking being.

**[Appendix, paragraph that begins "So far I seem to be attended"]**

So far I seem to be attended with sufficient evidence. But having thus loosened all our particular perceptions, when I proceed to explain the principle of connection, which binds them together, and makes us attribute to them a real simplicity and identity; I am sensible, that my account is very defective, and that nothing but the seeming evidence of the precedent reasonings could have induced me to receive it. If perceptions are distinct existences, they form a whole only by being connected together. But no connections among distinct existences are ever discoverable by human understanding. We only feel a connection or determination of the thought, to pass from one object to another. It follows, therefore, that the thought alone finds personal identity, when reflecting on the train of past perceptions, that compose a mind, the ideas of them are felt to be connected together, and naturally introduce each other. However extraordinary this conclusion may seem, it need not surprise us. Most philosophers seem inclined to think, that personal identity arises from consciousness; and consciousness is nothing but a reflected thought or perception. The present philosophy, therefore, has so far a promising aspect. But all my hopes vanish, when I come to explain the principles, that unite our successive perceptions in our thought or consciousness. I cannot discover any theory, which gives me satisfaction on this head.

**[Appendix, paragraph that begins "In short there are two principles"]**

In short there are two principles, which I cannot render consistent; nor is it in my power to renounce either of them, viz. that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connection among distinct existences. Did our perceptions either inhere in something simple and individual, or did the mind perceive some real connection among them, there would be no difficulty in the case. For my part, I must plead the privilege of a sceptic, and confess, that this difficulty is too hard for my understanding. I pretend not, however, to pronounce it absolutely insuperable. Others, perhaps, or myself, upon more mature reflections, may discover some hypothesis, that will reconcile those contradictions.

## Paragraphs

### P1

Hume’s account in Treatise 1.4.6 provides a stronger basis for rejecting an experientially warranted conception of a simple, enduring self than for accepting his explanation of the unity of perceptions. No invariant impression supplies the idea of such a self, and introspection discloses particular perceptions rather than a subject distinguishable from them (§6, paras. 2–3). Although Hume advances the stronger claim that the mind consists solely of successive perceptions, the failure to encounter a simple subject does not by itself establish that exhaustive account of its constitution (para. 4). His constructive explanation concerns identity as attributed in thought or imagination, explicitly leaving aside identity as it concerns the passions and self-concern (para. 5). Within this scope, memory reveals causal relations among perceptions and enables identity to be extended beyond remembered episodes; it therefore does not wholly constitute personal identity (para. 20). The gradual variation of relations and transitions also explains why precise boundaries of identity lack a determinate standard, making disputes over those boundaries grammatical rather than substantive (para. 21). The Appendix, however, exposes a difficulty deeper than uncertainty about where to draw such boundaries: Hume cannot satisfactorily explain how distinct perceptions are united in thought or consciousness when no real connection between distinct existences is perceived. This admission neither restores a substantial self nor simply retracts the preceding critique, whose apparent evidential force he continues to acknowledge. It nevertheless leaves the positive account insecure, since appealing to connected perceptions cannot complete the explanation while their connection in consciousness remains unresolved. The resulting position is thus an enduring challenge to the simple self accompanied by an acknowledged failure to explain fully the unity attributed to its perceptual succession.

### P2

Hume’s account of personal identity in Treatise 1.4.6 establishes that the self cannot be understood as a simple, enduring substance underlying our experiences, but must instead be conceived as a succession or bundle of distinct perceptions. His introspective argument is that whenever he examines what he calls himself, he encounters only particular perceptions, such as sensations, emotions, and thoughts, and never an impression of a constant and invariable self. The apparent continuity of personal identity is therefore explained not by an independently existing subject but by relations among successive perceptions, particularly resemblance and causation, which facilitate the imagination’s transition from one perception to another. Memory plays a central role in this process, not by producing identity itself, but by revealing connections among past perceptions and enabling us to extend the attribution of identity beyond what we explicitly remember. Since these relations admit of gradual variation, Hume further suggests that disputes about the precise boundaries of personal identity lack any determinate philosophical standard and are largely verbal. Nevertheless, the Appendix substantially qualifies the security of this account. Hume acknowledges that although he can establish the distinctness of individual perceptions, he cannot satisfactorily explain the principle that connects them into the unity we attribute to a mind. His difficulty arises from the apparent incompatibility of two commitments: that perceptions are distinct existences and that the mind cannot perceive any real connection among distinct existences. The relations that supposedly account for personal identity consequently risk explaining only the mind’s tendency to attribute unity, rather than establishing how such unity is possible. Hume therefore does not simply abandon his account, but confesses his inability to reconcile its underlying principles. The resulting position is best understood as a powerful critique of the substantial self and a provisional account of how personal identity is attributed, rather than a fully secured explanation of the unity and continuity of the mind.

### P3

Hume rejects a simple, enduring self: no constant, invariable impression could ground its idea, and introspection discloses only particular perceptions. He describes the mind as a succession or bundle, not a theatre-like container; only perceptions constitute it. This argument concerns identity as thought or imagination, not as passion or self-concern, and Hume allows that others may experience a simple, continued self. Identity is attributed through relations and ease of transition; as these fade by insensible degrees, no standard fixes when identity begins or ends, and disputes are therefore grammatical, except where relations of parts invite a fictional union. Memory chiefly discovers, not produces, identity: it supplies the notion of causation, which lets us extend the causal chain to forgotten times and actions. The Appendix does not withdraw the case against a simple self, but exposes the weakness of the connecting account. Hume says the earlier arguments still seem sufficiently evidenced, and that reflective thought finds identity as past ideas feel connected and naturally introduce one another. This is a “promising aspect,” but the mind feels only a transition, rather than perceiving a real connection among distinct existences; Hume calls his explanation very defective and unsatisfactory. He treats the difficulty as a reason for scepticism and modesty, not as absolutely insoluble.

### P4

Hume’s account challenges the idea of a simple, enduring self by denying its basis in an invariant impression and reporting that introspection encounters only particular perceptions (1.4.6.2–3). His ensuing bundle claim sets aside those professing a different experience, and the inquiry expressly addresses identity in thought and imagination rather than passionate self-concern (1.4.6.3–5). Memory reveals causal relations among perceptions, enabling identity to extend beyond remembered episodes rather than constituting it entirely (1.4.6.20). The theatre analogy supplies no underlying stage: Hume expressly disclaims knowledge of a place or material supporting the succession (1.4.6.4). His description of subtle identity disputes as grammatical rests on the absence of a precise standard for gradually varying relations and transitions, with an exception for relations generating an imagined principle of union (1.4.6.21). These considerations do not resolve the Appendix’s difficulty: Hume cannot reconcile perceptions’ distinct existence with the mind’s inability to perceive real connections among them, and finds no satisfactory account of their union in consciousness. His critique of the simple self therefore remains distinguishable from a successful explanation of personal unity. The Appendix supplies neither a reinstatement of a substantial bearer nor a declaration that reconciliation is impossible, but leaves that explanatory task open.

### P5

Hume’s account in Treatise 1.4.6 most securely undermines the claim that experience supplies an idea of a simple, enduring self; its positive explanation of personal identity remains less secure. Since no impression persists unchanged throughout life, and introspection reveals only particular perceptions, Hume finds no experiential basis for the invariant subject those perceptions supposedly belong to (§6, paras. 2–3). His description of the mind as a “bundle” accordingly excludes an independently apprehended bearer of perceptions, while his warning about the theatre metaphor prevents us from quietly restoring such a bearer as the stage on which they appear (para. 4). Yet these arguments do not by themselves establish the unrestricted metaphysical conclusion that no subject could exist: their immediate force concerns what experience reveals and what idea it can support. Hume’s further task is to explain our attribution of identity to changing perceptions through relations that facilitate transitions in thought. Memory reveals causal relations and enables us to extend personal identity beyond remembered episodes; it therefore cannot simply constitute identity through recollection alone (para. 20). The gradual weakening of these relations also leaves no precise standard for settling borderline identity claims (para. 21). The Appendix, however, exposes a difficulty deeper than such verbal indeterminacy: Hume cannot satisfactorily explain how distinct perceptions are united in thought or consciousness while maintaining that the mind perceives no real connection among distinct existences. This admission does not expressly withdraw his critique of a simple self, but it prevents the associative account from standing as a completed explanation of mental unity. The resulting position is thus asymmetrical: the challenge to an experientially warranted substantial self retains its stated grounds, whereas the explanation of how perceptions compose a mind remains explicitly unresolved.

### P6

Hume's account in Treatise 1.4.6 denies experiential grounding to the idea of a simple, invariant self: impressions vary, and introspection reveals particular perceptions. The mind is presented as their changing collection, without a separately conceived bearer or stage. Within his stated concern with identity in thought or imagination, rather than passionate self-concern, memory chiefly supplies access to the succession of perceptions and discovers causal relations; identity can consequently extend to forgotten periods. Remembering every episode is therefore not a requirement for attributing identity across time. He also explains the indeterminacy of subtle identity disputes through the gradual weakening of relations and of the transitions they facilitate: these supply no justified threshold for assigning the term identity. His characterization of those disputes as verbal does not settle every question about personhood. In the Appendix, however, Hume retains the distinct existence of perceptions and the mind's inability to perceive real connections between them, while finding his account of their connection defective and no theory that satisfies him. His refusal to declare the difficulty absolutely insuperable leaves open the possibility of a satisfactory hypothesis.
