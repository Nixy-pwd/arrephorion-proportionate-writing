### 2. Variants

**Direct**

Hume’s account challenges a simple, enduring self: no invariant impression supplies its idea, and introspection reveals particular perceptions rather than their bearer (1.4.6.2–4). His bundle claim expressly sets aside those claiming a different experience; his inquiry concerns identity in thought and imagination, not passionate self-concern (1.4.6.3–5). Memory discloses causal relations through which identity extends beyond remembered episodes, rather than wholly producing it (1.4.6.20). Yet the Appendix leaves the account of perceptual unity unresolved: Hume retains both the distinct existence of perceptions and our inability to perceive real connections among them, without reconciling them. The passages thus support a critique of the simple self, but not a secure explanation of personal unity; Hume neither restores a substantial self nor declares reconciliation impossible.

**Balanced**

Hume’s account challenges the idea of a simple, enduring self by denying its basis in an invariant impression and reporting that introspection encounters only particular perceptions (1.4.6.2–3). His ensuing bundle claim sets aside those professing a different experience, and the inquiry expressly addresses identity in thought and imagination rather than passionate self-concern (1.4.6.3–5). Memory reveals causal relations among perceptions, enabling identity to extend beyond remembered episodes rather than constituting it entirely (1.4.6.20). **The theatre analogy supplies no underlying stage: Hume expressly disclaims knowledge of a place or material supporting the succession (1.4.6.4).** **His description of subtle identity disputes as grammatical rests on the absence of a precise standard for gradually varying relations and transitions, with an exception for relations generating an imagined principle of union (1.4.6.21).** These considerations do not resolve the Appendix’s difficulty: Hume cannot reconcile perceptions’ distinct existence with the mind’s inability to perceive real connections among them, and finds no satisfactory account of their union in consciousness. His critique of the simple self therefore remains distinguishable from a successful explanation of personal unity. The Appendix supplies neither a reinstatement of a substantial bearer nor a declaration that reconciliation is impossible, but leaves that explanatory task open.

**Careful**

Hume’s account gives grounds for rejecting the idea of a simple, enduring self: no invariant impression supplies it, while introspection encounters particular perceptions rather than an independently apprehended bearer (1.4.6.2–3). The bundle claim nevertheless sets aside those claiming a different experience, and the inquiry concerns identity in thought and imagination, not passionate self-concern (1.4.6.3–5). Its theatre contains no independently known stage or supporting material (1.4.6.4). Memory reveals causal relations through which personal identity extends beyond remembered episodes; it does not produce identity entirely (1.4.6.20). Subtle identity disputes become grammatical because gradually varying relations and transitions supply no precise naming threshold, although Hume excepts relations giving rise to an imagined principle of union (1.4.6.21). **This diagnosis can be read as separating uncertainty about when to apply “identity” from the explanatory problem of how perceptions form one mind; resolving the former verbally would not by itself settle the latter.** That problem remains explicit in the Appendix: Hume retains perceptions’ distinct existence and the mind’s inability to perceive real connections among them, yet cannot reconcile these commitments or satisfactorily explain their union in consciousness. **His distinction between a felt transition of thought and a discoverable real connection suggests where the explanatory pressure falls: describing how perceptions introduce one another does not yet satisfy his demand for an account of their unity.** The critique of a simple self thus has an argumentative basis without delivering a secure positive account of personal unity. **Reading the Appendix as exposing this explanatory gap accommodates both his continuing confidence in the preceding reasoning and his confession of inconsistency.** It neither reinstates a substantial self nor rules out a future reconciliation.
